About the poem

Artist statement

In Hiligaynon, “kíkik” means cicada.

Being Filipino/Ilonggo-Japanese, I’ve moved repeatedly throughout my life, and have also been unable to move when I wanted to. I have experiences both as the one transferring and the one being left behind. Even when I wanted to leave a place, I’d catch myself in the middle of my thought and feeling, and think, this moment, this place, will never be again exactly what it is right now.

I recorded Platypleura fulvigera outside my former home near Diliman in the Philippines in 2016. I guess I was just collecting field recordings to use in music, but it was also a recording of that particular place and time, one that would become an archive of a place I’d left. A cicada chorus is specific to its trees and time of day. Diliman is one of Metro Manila’s greener areas, in a city built densely enough that green space has become scarce. I no longer live in that house, and I hear other noises, and sometimes other insects.

The noise around a place is part of what makes it that place, and it changes when you leave and when you come back. Cicadas in both Filipino and Japanese cultures act as clocks: they announce seasons, an hour of day, and the particular trees and heat of a place.

I wrote a poem in Hiligaynon, Japanese, and English, and used the cicada recording to make its electroacoustic and text versions.

Poem and translations
alas ocho nait is eight o’clock
夏ですit is summer
dandansoy, bayaan ta ikawDandansoy, I will leave you
1年one year
it’s 8 o’clock
summer na namanit is summer again

The third line quotes “Dandansoy,” a traditional Ilonggo folk song about leaving.

Musical process

I mapped the individual attacks of the cicada calls, and used them to trigger sound grains from my recorded poem. The swarm mediates the voice, like it speaks through me, or me through it.

Folded into the cicada chorus, the sung line from “Dandansoy” becomes part of an interspecies voice through which I revisit place and time.

Text process

The text on the page is made from the same cicada recording. My detector found 22,639 separate chirps in 4 minutes 43 seconds. I mapped the poem’s characters to these chirps computationally through granular synthesis.

The poem is a kind of grid in which each character cell or space stands for about a tenth of a second. A character is printed in a cell when it detects a chirp. The character comes from whichever line of the poem is being scanned at the moment, sampling a span of characters in a rolling window. The resulting characters are smeared and swarm-like across the text.
