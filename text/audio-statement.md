I am Filipino-Japanese and have moved repeatedly throughout my life. In both cultures, cicadas act as clocks: they announce seasons, an hour of day, and the particular trees and heat of a place. 

I recorded _Platypleura fulvigera_ outside my former home near Diliman in 2016, knowing that the recording would later become an archive of a place I’d left. Diliman remains one of Metro Manila’s greener areas. Where I live now, I no longer hear cicadas in the same way. I mapped the individual attacks of the cicada calls, and used them to trigger sound grains from my multilingual Ilonggo, Japanese, and English poem. The swarm mediates the voice, like it speaks through me, or me through it. 

_alas ocho na_ — it is eight o’clock 

夏です — it is summer _dandansoy, bayaan ta ikaw_ — Dandansoy, I will leave you _1_ 年 — one year _it’s 8 o’clock_ 

_summer na naman_ — it is summer again 

The sung third line quotes “Dandansoy,” a traditional Ilonggo folk song about leaving. Folded into the cicada chorus, this song of departure becomes part of an interspecies voice through which I revisit place and time.
